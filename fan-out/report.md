# Let a script, not Claude, run fan-outs

Anthropic's own documentation, the practitioner record and the research literature converge on one design: the orchestrating Claude should hold verdicts and file paths, never worker output, and for anything beyond a handful of workers the fan-out and merge should be done by a deterministic script (Claude Code's Workflow runtime) rather than by an LLM turn loop. Of the six candidate patterns, (a) summaries-plus-files and (b) scripted fan-out have direct first-party endorsement; (c) capping concurrency is forced on Max users regardless of design, because 2026 bug reports show server-side 429s starting at roughly **6 concurrent streams while plan usage reads 20–30%**; (d) hierarchical map-reduce is what the maintainers shipped (nesting depth 3) in answer to the "compaction tax" issue; (e) fresh briefed agents beat forks on cost for read-only work but are exactly where parallel writers conflict; and (f) short-lived orchestrators with file handoff is the community consensus, well-motivated but unmeasured. The typical stall (a session that once burned ~20M tokens in an hour or two now crawling at ~200k per 10 minutes) is not primarily context bloat: a bloated context raises tokens per minute, it does not collapse them. A collapsing token rate while "constantly stalling" is the signature of requests not completing, i.e. the client sitting in 429/529 retry back-off (up to 10 retries, 0.5–32 s each) across many concurrent workers, compounded by the 5-minute cache TTL that subagents get even on a subscription, by background completion notifications each re-sending the whole parent context, and by Fable drawing the usage window faster than other models. The recommendation is to adopt (a)+(b)+(c)+(f) now, with (d) as the extension when a wave's stubs are still too large and (e) restricted to readers; the evidence is convergent vendor guidance plus anecdote rather than controlled trials, but the mechanisms are documented and the cost of adopting is low.

## The crawl is retry back-off and cache churn, not window exhaustion

Start with what the client does on every turn. Claude Code "sends your full conversation with every request, and each time Claude uses tools it sends another request carrying that batch of tool results" ([Manage costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs#why-usage-climbs-in-a-long-session)). Subagents start in the background by default and their results "reach Claude as a completion notification in a later turn"; while fork mode is on (default since v2.1.302) Claude also "can't wait for a subagent" in an interactive session ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). So an orchestrator that spawns N workers ends its turn and then receives N notification turns, each of which re-sends the entire parent context. At a warm cache those re-sends are cheap and fast: cache reads cost **0.1x base, and only 0.025x on Fable 5.1** ([Prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)), and the token counter climbs rapidly because every cache read is still counted. That is how a healthy fan-out shows tens of millions of tokens in an hour: the number is dominated by cheap, fast prefix re-reads over a large, warm context.

The same arithmetic inverts when requests stop completing. Claude Code retries transient failures "up to 10 times with exponential backoff", with 529 back-off starting at 500 ms and rising to 32,000 ms ([Errors](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/errors); [Env vars](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/env-vars)). A worker in back-off produces no tokens and no progress; a dozen of them produce a session that looks alive in the Background tasks pane but moves at a crawl. The 2026 record on Max accounts is consistent and specific: issue #96872 measured **14.3% of session-minutes containing a 429 at 6 or more concurrent desktop sessions versus 0–2.2% at 1–5**, with plan usage at 27% of the 5-hour window and 26% weekly ([#96872](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/96872)); issue #53922 captured 88 consecutive 429s over about 15 minutes, each silently retried about six times, on a Max 20x account at 20% session usage ([#53922](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/53922)); issue #60562 reports "just 2–5 parallel subagents" hitting 429s with Opus ([#60562](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/60562)). The message these users see is "Server is temporarily limiting requests (not your usage limit)", and Anthropic's errors page confirms that a 529 "is not your usage limit and doesn't count against your quota" ([Errors](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/errors)). None of these issues received a maintainer response and all were closed by the stale bot, so there is no official statement of a per-plan concurrency ceiling; the practical ceiling Max users report is single-digit concurrent streams, far below Claude Code's own client-side cap of 20 ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). Peak-hour throttling adds a second layer: TechRadar reported, with confirmation from a Claude Code engineer, that 5-hour limits drain faster during weekday peak hours of 5–11am PT ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/ai-platforms-assistants/claude/claude-is-limiting-usage-more-aggressively-during-peak-hours-heres-what-changed)).

Three further mechanisms turn a slow fan-out into an expensive one. First, cache lifetime: on a Claude subscription the main conversation gets a one-hour TTL, but "everything else" (subagents, workflows, forks, compaction) gets **five minutes** unless `subagentPromptCacheTtl: "1h"` is set ([Prompt caching in Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching#which-ttl-each-request-gets)). A worker that sits in back-off or blocks on a slow tool for more than five minutes re-reads its whole context uncached on its next request, and the Claude Code team's own blog warns that "a few percentage points of cache miss rate can dramatically affect cost and latency" because a miss re-bills the entire prefix at full price ([Prompt caching is everything](https://claude.dev/blog/lessons-from-building-claude-code-prompt-caching-is-everything/)). Second, on the API only uncached input counts toward input-token rate limits, so each fresh subagent's first request (entirely `cache_creation_input_tokens`) is the most rate-limit-hungry request in the system, while the orchestrator's repeated prefix is nearly free ([Rate limits](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits#cache-aware-itpm)); whether the subscription meter discounts cache reads the same way is not documented. Third, model choice: Anthropic's support article says Fable models "draw from your plan's regular weekly usage limits and use them faster than other Claude models" ([Claude Fable models on your plan](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/15424964-claude-fable-models-on-your-plan)), which matters on any Max plan where the 5-hour window is the binding constraint.

Context bloat is real but it is the slower, second-order failure. Issue #23463 shows the hard version: seven parallel subagents returned 15–37K characters each (~150K total), after which the parent answered "Prompt is too long" to every message including the pending agent notifications, and the session was unrecoverable ([#23463](https://claudeissues.com/issue/23463-subagent-results-silently-overflow-context-causing-unrecoverable-session-crash)). Issue #19077 shows the soft version: a 9-hour orchestrator that kept every phase's intermediate output suffered "repeated compaction" that "degraded the orchestrator's memory of early decisions", a cost its author named the compaction tax and modelled as session length times intermediate-output size ([#19077](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/19077)). And there is a third stall mode that looks identical from the outside: issue #86200 counted **31 of 188 background-task notifications dropped** on v2.1.218, 11 from subagents that had completed successfully, so a parent waiting on a lost notification is indistinguishable from a parent waiting on a hung worker unless you open the transcript file ([#86200](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/86200)). The session's own `/usage` line (v2.1.251+) reports cache hit share, warm/cold state and the likely cause of the last miss ([Manage costs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/costs#prompt-cache-statistics)); it is the one tool that will tell you, for a specific crawling session, whether you are paying for misses, retries or both.

## Anthropic's escalation ladder ends at a script, not an orchestrator

Anthropic's documentation now describes an explicit tiering. Subagents keep "intermediate results" in "Claude's context window" and suit "a few delegated tasks per turn"; agent teams keep them in a shared task list; workflows keep them in "script variables" and scale to "dozens to hundreds of agents per run" ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows)). The architectural statement is unambiguous: with subagents "Claude is the orchestrator: it decides turn by turn what to spawn or assign next, and every result goes into a context window. A workflow script holds the loop, the branching, and the intermediate results itself, so Claude's context holds only the final answer" ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows)). The Agent SDK repeats it: "Subagents work well for a few delegated tasks per turn. For runs that coordinate dozens to hundreds of agents, use the Workflow tool" ([Subagents in the SDK](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/subagents)). This is pattern (b) as a shipped product feature, and it is the strongest endorsement any of the six patterns has.

The workflow runtime also absorbs the failure modes that make an LLM orchestrator stall. It runs **up to 16 concurrent agents by default** (fewer with fewer CPUs; `CLAUDE_CODE_WORKFLOW_MAX_CONCURRENT_AGENTS` 1–256), holds same-prefix siblings for up to 5,000 ms so they read the first agent's cache, restarts "an agent whose output stops arriving for long enough" from the same prompt up to five times, pauses the run when an agent hits the claude.ai usage limit and resumes after reset (at most twice per run), and replays saved results on relaunch so only failed agents and those after them re-run ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows#behavior-and-limits)). A `schema` on `agent()` makes a worker "return JSON matching the shape instead of prose" ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows)). The trade-offs are also documented: no mid-run user input, a `Large workflow` warning past 25 agents or a projected 1.5M tokens, a size guideline that defaults to "medium" (fewer than 10 agents), and the advice to "run the workflow on a small slice first" ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows#cost)). The Claude Platform docs give the same shape outside Claude Code: an orchestration-mode example where code (a thread pool, `MAX_CONCURRENT=10`, `MAX_TOTAL_SUBTASKS=200`, 15 turns per subagent) does the fan-out, each subagent calls `report_findings` exactly once with a 2–3 sentence summary plus structured findings, and results are journalled to `orchestration_journal.json` keyed by prompt hash so "only the subtasks that never finished are recomputed" ([Build an orchestration mode](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/mid-conversation-effort-example)).

Pattern (a) is the second tier of the same ladder and the oldest piece of first-party advice. The 2025 research-system post describes subagents returning condensed findings and, in its appendix, storing outputs "in external systems" and passing "lightweight references back to the coordinator" ([Multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). The context-engineering post gives the only first-party size target: a subagent may burn "tens of thousands of tokens or more" but returns "only a condensed, distilled summary", typically **1,000–2,000 tokens** ([Effective context engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-context-engineering-for-ai-agents)). The Claude Code docs state the warning ("Running many subagents that each return detailed results can consume significant context") and the fix ("Ask for short summaries back, so twenty results don't fill your context anyway") ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents); [Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). What the docs do not provide is any enforcement: there is no cap on an Agent-tool result, it is injected as an ordinary tool result rather than spilled to a file the way Bash output over ~30,000 characters is ([Tools reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/tools-reference)), and the "write to file, return path" convention is user guidance, not a feature. Its known failure path is the fallback: issue #44657 describes a worker whose Write call was refused and which then returned the full content as text, "which consumes parent context budget for content that was specifically meant to be persisted to a file" ([#44657](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/44657)). A PostToolUse hook on the Agent tool with `updatedToolOutput` is the only documented mechanism that could bound the injected result ([Hooks](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/hooks)), but the docs do not confirm it applies to the Agent result shape, so it needs a test before you rely on it.

On concurrency (c), Anthropic's numbers point the same direction from three different products. The research system's lead prompt scales effort as **1 agent for simple fact-finding, 2–4 subagents for comparisons, 10+ only for complex research**, and its authors admit early versions were "spawning 50 subagents for simple queries" ([Multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Agent teams guidance says "Start with 3-5 teammates for most workflows" and "Three focused teammates often outperform five scattered ones" ([Agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)). The best-practices page retired "use subagents to protect your context window" on the grounds that "Running out of context is rarely the problem anymore" with 1M windows and auto-compaction, and reframed subagents as costing "mostly tokens, since every subagent sends its own requests", giving "parallelism or a fresh pair of eyes out of it, but not a smarter answer" ([Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). The Agent SDK adds that Opus 5 "delegates to subagents more readily than earlier models", which is why the caps "matter most" there ([Subagents in the SDK](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/subagents)); a Hacker News thread on Opus 5 being hard-coded not to spawn subagents reads the same way ([HN 49056022](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49056022)).

## Research backs thin coordinators, but not with the experiment one would want

The literature supplies the why behind (a) and (d), and it is unambiguous that a long context is a liability even when nothing is lost. Lost in the Middle found GPT-3.5-Turbo multi-document QA falling from **75.8% with the answer first to 53.8% in the middle** of 20 documents, below its closed-book score, and that going from 20 to 50 retrieved documents bought about 1.5 points for a large increase in length, latency and cost ([Liu et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). NoLiMa, peer-reviewed at ICML 2025, showed that once lexical overlap is removed **10 of 12 models fall below 50% of their short-context baseline at 32K tokens**, GPT-4o from 99.3% to 69.7% ([Modarressi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167)). Du et al. (Findings of EMNLP 2025) is the most pointed: performance "still degrades substantially (13.9%–85%)" as input grows even when all relevant evidence is retrieved and even when the irrelevant tokens are replaced by whitespace ([Du et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05381)). Chroma's Context Rot report, across 18 models including the Claude 4 family, found every model significantly better on a ~300-token focused prompt than the ~113k-token full version of the same LongMemEval question, with the Claude family showing the largest gap, mostly as abstentions ([Chroma 2025](https://www.trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). Each worker transcript appended to an orchestrator is a distractor set for every later decision; that LongMemEval contrast is the closest published analogue of "full worker output versus condensed summary in the coordinator". The multi-turn study by Laban et al. adds the orchestrator-specific regime: an average **39% drop from single-turn to multi-turn** on the same tasks, mostly a reliability loss where models "take a wrong turn… and do not recover" ([Laban et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06120)).

Offloading state is the best-evidenced single remedy. MemGPT lifted GPT-4's deep-memory retrieval from **32.1% to 92.5%** by paging facts in on demand instead of holding them in context, and was unaffected by document count where fixed-context GPT-4 degraded ([Packer et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08560)). OpenAI's recursive summarization (summaries of summaries, pattern (d) in its purest form) reached state of the art on BookSum and NarrativeQA while letting labelers supervise without reading the whole book ([Wu et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.10862)). The Berkeley MAST taxonomy (NeurIPS 2025), built from 150+ traces across seven frameworks with **41–86.7% failure rates**, found the dominant modes are step repetition (15.7%), reasoning-action mismatch (13.2%), unaware of termination (12.4%) and disobeying the task spec (11.8%), i.e. what an overloaded or under-briefed coordinator produces, and concluded that topology changes beat prompt changes while even the best interventions only added 9–16 points ([Cemri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v3)). Google and MIT's scaling study is the strongest number for a central coordinator: independent agents **amplified errors 17.2x versus 4.4x under centralized coordination**, centralized multi-agent gained 80.9% on a parallelizable finance task, and every multi-agent variant degraded a sequential planning task by 39–70% ([Kim et al. 2025](https://research.google/blog/towards-a-science-of-scaling-agent-systems-when-and-why-agent-systems-work/)).

Two places the evidence is contested or thin deserve explicit weight. The headline case for fan-out, Anthropic's 90.2% improvement of an Opus 4 lead with Sonnet 4 subagents over single Opus 4, is an internal eval uncontrolled for compute, and the same post reports that on BrowseComp "token usage by itself explains 80% of the variance" ([Multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Tran and Kiela (April 2026) argue from the data-processing inequality that single agents are more information-efficient and show them matching or beating several multi-agent architectures under equal reasoning-token budgets, with API budget artifacts inflating apparent multi-agent gains ([Tran & Kiela 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)); vendor-reported BrowseComp numbers for the same model single versus multi show modest gains of 2–5 points ([steel.dev leaderboard](https://leaderboard.steel.dev/leaderboards/browsecomp)). The honest reading is that fan-out wins on breadth-first, read-heavy, parallelizable work (a documentation audit is exactly this) and merely matches at higher cost, or loses, on sequential and write-heavy work. Cognition's "Don't Build Multi-Agents" is the canonical warning for the write side ("actions carry implicit decisions"), and their April 2026 follow-up says most working deployments limit subagents to read-only search ([Cognition 2025](https://cognition.com/blog/dont-build-multi-agents); [Cognition 2026](https://cognition.com/blog/multi-agents-working)); Harrison Chase's reconciliation, that "conflicting writes are costlier than conflicting reads", is the position most practitioners now cite ([LangChain 2025](https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-and-when-to-build-multi-agent-systems)). The second thin spot is structured handoffs: the only controlled study found strict JSON-mode output cut GSM8K reasoning accuracy from **86.5% to 23.4% on Claude 3 Haiku** while helping classification, with "reason in prose, then convert" recovering the loss ([Tam et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.02442v1)). A schema on the worker's return is a parse-reliability and brief-completeness lever, not an accuracy lever, and no study measures schema-gated handoffs reducing cascade errors. Finally, no paper anywhere runs the A/B that would settle it, orchestrator-receives-transcript versus orchestrator-receives-summary-plus-path; the case for (a) rests on the degradation curves above plus Anthropic's production report, which is strong mechanism evidence and weak outcome evidence.

## Pattern verdicts and how practitioners combine them

| Pattern | Evidence | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (a) Orchestrator reads 1–2k-token stubs; workers write raw output to files | First-party target size and "lightweight references"; no enforcement; fallback dumps text into parent | Adopt, with a fallback instruction and a size cap in every brief |
| (b) Deterministic Workflow script does fan-out and merge | Shipped feature with documented stall restart, usage-limit pause, cache stagger, resumable journal; no mid-run input | Adopt for anything over ~5–10 workers |
| (c) Cap concurrency / batch into waves | Max 429s at ~6 concurrent streams measured by users; Anthropic's own 2–4 / 3–5 sizing; no official ceiling | Mandatory: 3–5 concurrent, staggered |
| (d) Hierarchical map-reduce | Maintainers shipped nesting depth 3 in answer to #19077; MemGPT and recursive summarization numbers | Use only when a wave's stubs exceed the parent's budget |
| (e) Fresh briefed agents instead of forks | Fresh agents skip parent bloat but pay 10–30k startup each; forks share cache but copy bloat; Cognition's write-conflict warning | Fresh for readers; one agent, not a fan-out, for writes |
| (f) Short-lived orchestrators, file-based handoff | Ralph loop, HANDOFF.md consensus; Anthropic's "spawn fresh subagents with clean contexts"; unmeasured | Adopt as phase boundaries, with `/clear` between phases |

The practitioner record supports (a) and (f) as a pair. Issue #19077's author proposed depth-0 holding only phase verdicts, depth-1 holding working state, and depth-2 doing the fan-out "so intermediates are discarded with the worker", and the maintainer closed it when v2.1.219 shipped nested subagents with a default depth of 3 ([#19077](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/19077)); that is pattern (d) adopted as the product's structural answer to the compaction tax. The named file conventions are `HANDOFF.md` ("CLAUDE.md describes the project structure and conventions, while HANDOFF.md describes the current state of work") and `memory/handoff-[date].md` ([fazm.ai](https://fazm.ai/blog/claude-code-architecture-handoff-pattern); [claudecodeguide.dev](https://claudecodeguide.dev/blog/handoff-protocol-saves-10-minutes)), Manus's rewritten `todo.md` that keeps the plan floating at the end of context and treats the file system as "the ultimate context… unlimited in size, persistent by nature" ([Manus 2025](https://manus.im/blog/Context-Engineering-for-AI-Agents-Lessons-from-Building-Manus)), and the Ralph loop's fresh context per iteration with "state in files, not conversation", which its documenters note is both why it works and why it is expensive ([codecentric](https://codecentric.de/en/knowledge-hub/blog/the-ralph-wiggum-loop-autonomous-code-generation-with-a-fresh-context)). The projects whose authors report concrete lessons all moved coordination out of the LLM: a shell loop, a deterministic script that "passed each step to a fresh agent and committed after each one" ([HN 45181577](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=45181577)), or a Redis queue with per-file locks where commenters pushed for "a deterministic coordinator, not an AI agent" for merges ([Redmond 2025](https://dev.to/bredmond1019/multi-agent-orchestration-running-10-claude-instances-in-parallel-part-3-29da)). Where the measured numbers exist they are single anecdotes: one guide reports the main session at 56% of context versus 18% for the orchestrator when the same work went to subagents, with no method described ([dplooy.com](https://www.dplooy.com/blog/claude-code-tasks-complete-guide-to-ai-agent-workflow)), and one developer measured a Pro plan exhausting its window in ~15 minutes with five parallel subagents versus ~30 minutes sequentially, "quota drains proportionally to concurrency" ([Cheng 2025](https://dev.to/onlineeric/claude-code-sub-agents-burn-out-your-tokens-4cd8)).

Pattern (e) needs the sharpest qualification. A fresh subagent "doesn't see your conversation history, the skills you've already invoked, or the files Claude has already read", so the brief must carry everything; a fork "inherits the entire conversation so far" and reads the parent's cache, so it "costs less than a fresh subagent for tasks that need the same context" ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents); [Prompt caching in Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching#subagents-and-the-cache)). The practitioner cost of fresh agents is the startup prefix: HN commenters report each subagent sending roughly a 30k-token system prompt and re-reading code that sequential work would have hit in cache, and one user watched seven subagents use up the budget "before any of them finished" on a task that ran fine sequentially ([HN 48883796](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48883796)). So the choice is not fresh-versus-fork in the abstract: fresh agents are right when the parent is already large (a fork would copy the bloat and re-send it on every worker turn) and when the work is read-only; forks are right when the parent is small and the worker needs the conversation's decisions. Anthropic's research system is the model: a lead that delegates reading to parallel workers and then lets "a single agent" write the final report ([Multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)).

## What to change in practice

For a large read-heavy job such as a documentation audit, on a Max plan in the desktop app, the design that follows from all of the above is a thin, short-lived orchestrator over a scripted, wave-limited fan-out of read-only workers, with one agent doing the writes. Concretely: brief each worker with the file paths to read, the exact output file to write under a run directory, and a return contract of "one paragraph plus the path, under 1,500 tokens, and if the Write fails say so and return the path you tried rather than the content"; that last clause is the only defence against the #44657 fallback. Keep total agents at whatever the audit needs, but hold **concurrent streams to 3–5 per wave with starts staggered by a few seconds**, which is the practical Max ceiling users measured and the sizing Anthropic gives for teams ([#96872](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/96872); [Agent teams](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-teams)); a rule of thumb such as "fan-outs under ~40 agents" bounds the wrong variable, since the throttle is on concurrency, not totals. For a run of more than about ten workers, let the Workflow runtime own the loop, with a `schema` on each `agent()` call for the stub and prose reasoning inside the worker, run it on a small slice first, and accept that you cannot steer mid-run; its 16-agent cap is a ceiling, not a target, so set `CLAUDE_CODE_WORKFLOW_MAX_CONCURRENT_AGENTS` to 4 or 5 on this account ([Dynamic workflows](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/workflows#behavior-and-limits)). The merge should be code where it can be (concatenating stubs, checking that every expected file exists) and a single fresh agent reading the files where judgment is needed, never the orchestrator reading all the raw output.

Set the settings that remove the documented self-inflicted costs. `subagentPromptCacheTtl: "1h"` stops workers re-warming their caches after a five-minute stall, at the price of 2x write cost ([Prompt caching in Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching#choose-the-ttl-yourself)). `CLAUDE_CODE_MAX_CONCURRENT_SUBAGENTS=5` makes the client refuse a sixth concurrent Agent-tool worker instead of letting Opus 5's eagerness to delegate run into the throttle ([Env vars](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/env-vars)). Give readers `model: haiku` or Sonnet and a `maxTurns`, and prefer the built-in Explore agent for pure lookups because it "starts without your CLAUDE.md files and the git status snapshot to keep research fast and inexpensive" ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). Never switch model, effort or fast mode in the orchestrating session mid-run, since each invalidates the cache and re-bills the full prefix ([Prompt caching in Claude Code](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/prompt-caching#actions-that-invalidate-the-cache)). Between audit phases, write the handoff file and `/clear` rather than `/compact`: `/clear` "costs nothing" while `/compact` "is itself a big request" that reads the whole conversation, and more so once the cache is cold ([Best practices](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)). When a session crawls, open `/usage` before anything else; a cold cache with a tool-list or compaction cause means churn, a high hit rate with no progress means back-off, and a worker that shows running with no turns for many minutes should be checked in its transcript file under `~/.claude/projects/{project}/{sessionId}/subagents/` rather than waited on ([Subagents](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sub-agents)). Schedule the heavy runs outside 5–11am Pacific.

The trade-offs are honest ones. The scripted design gives up conversational steering and the ability to redirect a worker mid-flight, which Anthropic itself lists as the known limitation of its synchronous lead ([Multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Waves of 3–5 make a 40-worker audit take eight to thirteen rounds instead of two, and because Max's 5-hour window is drained by total tokens across concurrent requests, the parallel version finishes sooner on the clock but not cheaper on the meter. Fresh workers each pay a 10–30k-token prefix that a fork would have shared. And the pattern's evidence base is convergent rather than controlled: every vendor, framework and practitioner source found points the same way, the degradation curves and the MemGPT numbers give the mechanism, but no one has run the matched comparison of these six patterns, the Max concurrency throttle is inferred from user-filed issues that Anthropic never answered, and the PostToolUse enforcement path is untested on Agent results. Those are reasons to measure your own runs (tokens per wave, 429s per minute from the Background tasks pane, cache hit share from `/usage`) and to record the numbers, not reasons to wait.

## Conclusion

The useful reframing is that "context bloat" and "rate limiting" are one problem seen from two sides: a fat orchestrator makes every notification turn and every cache miss larger, and a wide fan-out multiplies the requests that carry those turns, so the two failures feed each other until the client spends its time in back-off. Anthropic's product direction confirms the diagnosis by where it put the fixes: nesting depth so intermediates die with the worker, a workflow runtime so results never enter the conversation, per-subagent cache TTLs and compaction windows, and a usage-limit pause that only the runtime can perform. The LLM orchestrator was never meant to be the accumulator, and the retired best-practices advice ("use subagents to protect your context window") signals that Anthropic now sees the cost as tokens and cache misses rather than the window itself.

What the evidence does not yet settle is how much quality a thin coordinator buys relative to a well-compacted single agent on this specific kind of work; Tran and Kiela's budget-matched results and Anthropic's own "token usage explains 80%" finding both say that a large share of multi-agent gains is simply more compute. For a documentation audit that is fine: the goal is wall-clock coverage of many independent files under a hard usage window, which is the one regime where every source agrees fan-out pays, provided the reads are parallel, the writes are serial, and the coordinator holds nothing it did not need to decide.
